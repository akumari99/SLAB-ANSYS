Cross-check between the two cells

Take the shallow cell, carry it to the surface, then carry that back down to the deep cell, and compare with what the deep cell actually read. A large gap means the load is not arriving where the model says it is.

Converting between stiffness measures

Every arrow here is a correlation, not a law. Carry the source with the number.

k to E

E = 20.15 k^1.284, FAA AC 150/5320-6G. Approximate, adequate for design.

CBR to modulus

FAA E = 1500 CBR. MEPDG Mr = 2555 CBR^0.64. They disagree by up to a factor of two.

Static to cyclic k

Backcalculated dynamic k runs about twice static plate load k. AASHTO 1993 divides by two before design.

Modulus to k, three ways

FAA inverts its own power law. AASHTO 1993 uses k = Mr/19.4 for a composite k on the subgrade. The rigid plate solution k = E/(2a(1-u^2)) is exact for a rigid circular plate on a half space and shows why k depends on the size of the loaded area, which is the reason a plate load k and a backcalculated k are not the same number.

Static plate k, cyclic k and DCP derived Mr are three different quantities. Report them side by side. Never average them.

Stress state at the soil cell

You measure vertical stress where the cell sits, so the universal model can be evaluated at the real stress state instead of an assumed one.

Model
Mr = k1 pa (theta/pa)^k2 (tau_oct/pa + 1)^k3, pa = 14.7 psi
theta = sigma_v + 2 sigma_h (induced + geostatic), tau_oct = (sqrt2/3)(s1 - s3)

k1, k2, k3 come from an AASHTO T307 fit on your own material. Until you run T307, this returns a number with no provenance. Do not call it a resilient modulus in a paper without the test.

Equations, with the source for each

Westergaard 1926, Public Roads 7(2)

Eq 1 l = [E h³ / (12(1−μ²) k)]^¼
Eq 15 z_e = (1/√6)(1 + 0.4μ) P / (k l²)
Eq 16 z_e = 0.433 P / (k l²) [μ = 0.15]
Eq 17 k l² = √( E h³ k / (12(1−μ²)) )
Eq 18 k = 12(1−μ²)(k l²)² / (E h³)

Your sheet uses Eq 16 then Eq 18. Note that Eq 15 is stated as approximate for a concentrated force at the edge, so it carries no correction for the size of the loaded area.

Westergaard 1948, ASCE Trans 113, Paper 2340

Eq 13 σ_e, Case 3, ellipse tangent to the joint
Eq 14 z_e = P√(2+1.2μ)/√(E h³ k) [1−(0.76+0.4μ)b/l][1−(0.76+0.4μ)y/l]
Eq 19 z_j − z'_j = (1 − j)(z_e − z'_e)
Eq 20 σ_j = (1 − j/2) σ_e + (j/2) σ'_e
Eq 22 z_j = (1 − j/2) z_e + (j/2) z'_e
Eq 23 z'_j = (j/2) z_e + (1 − j/2) z'_e

With the load entirely on one side, z'_e = 0, so Eq 22 reduces to z_e = z_j/(1 − j/2), which is what your sheet does. Dividing Eq 23 by Eq 22 also gives j = 2R/(1+R) with R = z'_j/z_j, so j is measurable rather than assumed.

Two cautions straight from your own references. Westergaard 1926 p. 25 notes that plate bearing tests give a k that varies with the loaded area, roughly inversely with its square root, so a plate k and a slab k are not interchangeable. And Westergaard's closing reply in the 1948 paper, p. 444, says k is a property of the subgrade, the pavement, and the position of the load together, so the right k for an edge may differ from the right k for the interior by a factor between one and two.

k from the load-deflection slope

Seating of the rubber pad, bedding of the plate and any zero drift all show up as a constant offset in both pressure and deflection. The slope of the two against each other throws that offset away. This is the most robust k you can get from a static ramp.

Interface stress against deflection

A straight line means linear k. Curvature means stress dependent stiffness, which is the honest result for a compacted A-4.

k from one measured deflection

Invert the closed form for the case you loaded. One sensor, one number, no basin needed. Useful as a first guess and as a check that the basin fit has not wandered off.

Deflection against k

Your measured deflection is the dashed line. Where it crosses the curve is the answer.

Fit k to the whole basin

Least squares over k with E fixed from cylinder tests. The basin shape is the exact Kelvin function solution for a plate on a Winkler foundation, scaled to the Westergaard magnitude for the case you loaded, with load transfer applied across the joint.

Error surface

A flat bottom means the fit is not unique. A sharp minimum means k is well constrained by this basin.

Measured against fitted basin

k from the shape of the basin

The normalised basin of a plate on a Winkler foundation is a single universal curve in r/l. So the offset at which deflection has halved fixes l on its own, with no assumption about load magnitude. Then k follows from the centre deflection.

w(r) = -P kei(r/l) / (2 pi k l^2), kei(0) = -pi/4
w(r50)/w(0) = 0.5 at r50/l = 1.3018

Normalised basin

Markers are your sensors normalised by the peak. The curve is the theoretical shape at the fitted l. Markers that sit above the curve at distance mean the foundation is stiffer than Winkler allows, which is the usual sign that an elastic solid model fits better.

Subgrade modulus from a far sensor

Far enough from the load, the slab no longer matters and the deflection is governed by the subgrade alone. Boussinesq for a point load on a half space inverts directly. This is forwardcalculation, not iteration, and it is the standard screening check in the FHWA guidance.

E = (1 - u^2) P / (pi r d_r)

Modulus from each sensor

Run every sensor through the same equation. The far ones should converge on one value. Where they stop converging is where the slab still matters, and that offset tells you the real extent of the load spread.

k from bottom of slab strain

A completely independent route. Strain gives you stress, stress against k is a known curve, so k follows without using a single deflection. If this agrees with the deflection based methods you have a strong result.

Stress against k

Notice how flat this curve is. Stress barely moves over a wide band of k, which is exactly why a strain based k has a wide error bar and a strain based stress check is trustworthy.

Aggregate and soil moduli from the pressure cells

Two cells and one deflection give three equations for two moduli. Odemark transform for the layer contrast, Boussinesq for the stress, and integration of vertical strain down to the pit floor for the deflection. The pit floor is real, so the integration is bounded rather than a half space.

Fit against the measurements

This is the roughest method in the set. The strain integration assumes one dimensional compression on the load axis, which overstates deflection near the surface. Treat the modulus ratio as the useful output and the absolute values as indicative.

Import an ILLI-SLAB sweep

Run ILLI-SLAB once per k in a batch, pull the nodal deflection at each LVDT, paste the table here. The fit then interpolates that surface instead of calling the finite element code, so it is instant and it uses the right forward model for a finite doweled slab.

Batch script sketch for the ILLI-SLAB side

Compile the source with gfortran, write the input deck from a template with k as the only variable, loop k from 100 to 1200 pci at 50 pci steps, parse nodal deflections at your sensor coordinates, write one CSV row per k. Twenty minutes of Fortran wrangling buys you a response surface you reuse for every load step and every cycle count.

AREA, for the day you run an interior load

Four sensors at 0, 12, 24 and 36 inches from an interior load. Two extra LVDTs on the next pour unlock this as an independent check on the finite element fit.

AREA36 = 6[1 + 2(d12/d0) + 2(d24/d0) + d36/d0]. For a slab on a dense liquid with no bedrock AREA36 tends to 11.04 as the foundation stiffens. l comes from the Hall 1992 fit, then k from the non-dimensional interior deflection coefficient.

Joint & fatigue

Load transfer, impulse stiffness, and how both drift with cycles.

Load transfer efficiency

Deflection LTE = w unloaded / w loaded. Stress LTE is lower than deflection LTE for the same joint; FAA AC 150/5370-11B gives the relationship for a 12 inch plate.

Impulse stiffness

ISM = P / w0. A blunt single number that is useful for tracking one slab against itself over a test, and useless for comparing slabs of different thickness.

Drift with cycles

The plot that carries the flood resilience argument: foundation stiffness against cycle count, split by moisture state.

If k falls and LTE holds, the foundation is softening. If LTE falls and k holds, the dowels are loosening. Those are different failure mechanisms and a reviewer will ask which one you are looking at.

Keep it in Chrome

Three ways, cheapest first.

Bookmark this page. It is already a live URL. Your inputs save to the artifact store, so they come back on the next visit from any machine you are signed in on. Nothing to install.

Run it from a file. Download the HTML from the chat, drop it anywhere on disk, double click. Chrome opens it from file:// and it works offline. In that mode it saves to browser storage instead, so the data lives on that one machine and that one browser profile. Ctrl+D to pin it, or use Chrome's Save and share, Cast, save, and share, Install page as app to get it its own window and taskbar icon.

Put it on GitHub Pages. Push the single HTML file to a repo as index.html, turn on Pages in the repo settings, and you get a permanent URL you can send to your advisor. Same file, no build step, no server.

The three modes do not share data. If you switch, export the JSON from here and restore it on the other side.

What this does not do

It does not run ILLI-SLAB or FAARFIELD. Neither will run in a browser. The Fortran belongs in a local Python wrapper; this page is the front end for the numbers that come out of it. Same for AASHTO T307: the k1, k2, k3 fit is lab work, this only evaluates the model.

It does not read your raw DAQ text files directly. Peak extraction stays in the Python pipeline; paste or import the peaks table.
